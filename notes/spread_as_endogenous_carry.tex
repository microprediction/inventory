\begin{abstract}
Storage economics stabilizes inventory with a stick: it costs money to hold stock. For some goods the stick is weak, and inventory may become poorly anchored. We ask whether dealer quotes provide an endogenous stabilizing channel. The corrected Cotton--Papanicolaou symmetry gives one exact statement: flow imbalance translates skew and slows the effective inventory clock, so any \emph{existing} calendar-time carrying cost is amplified per inventory transition. It does not create a positive cost when carrying cost is identically zero, and the quote-width response must be obtained from the balanced problem at the amplified cost. We then separate that theorem from two conjectural steps: whether low-carry quote surfaces approach a constant-width, linear-skew regime, and whether the resulting dealer feedback closes into amplitude-dependent damping. The note is therefore a mechanism proposal and research scoreboard, not a proof of explosive inventory or a limit cycle.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem: nothing stops you}

A storable good is held in check from below and from above. Below, you cannot hold negative stock: at stockout the convenience yield goes vertical and the price spikes. Above, warehouses send invoices: carrying stock costs interest, insurance, and rent, so somebody has to expect appreciation to hold it, and that expectation had better come true on average.

Now delete the invoice. For high-value, non-degrading goods the all-in physical carry can be of order 20 basis points a year. At that price the classical argument for well-behaved inventory --- Weymar discarded the explosive root of his inventory dynamics on the ground that nobody carries losing stock forever --- has almost nothing to push against. Twenty basis points buys a great deal of ``forever.''

So what, in practice, stops a hoard? Not the warehouse. The honest answer is the trading desk: the cost that actually bites is price risk while you hold, funding against the position, and --- above all --- the terms on which you can get out. Every one of those is a property of the \emph{dealer market}, and the dealer market summarizes them in two numbers it quotes every day: width and skew. The thesis of this note, in one line:

\begin{equation}
\textit{The bid--offer is the cost of carry the physical world forgot to charge.}
\label{eq:slogan}
\end{equation}

\section{Storage in one page}\label{sec:storage}

Coverage is stock over consumption rate, $Y_t = I_t/q_t$ --- always time units, never tonnes. Weymar's storage relation says the expected log-price curve $\pi(t,h) = \mathbb{E}_t[\ln P_{t+h}]$ has slope
\begin{equation}
\partial_h \pi(t,h) = f\big(Y(t,h)\big),
\label{eq:storage}
\end{equation}
where $Y(t,h)$ is coverage expected at horizon $h$ and $f$ is increasing, concave, and flat where stocks are comfortable. Slopes are not levels; integrating against the long-run anchor $A_t$ (the price the market believes in once coverage normalizes) gives
\begin{equation}
\ln P_t = \ln A_t - \int_0^\infty f\big(Y(t,s)\big)\,ds .
\label{eq:price}
\end{equation}
In English: the spot price is the anchor, discounted by every bit of expected tightness along the whole expected coverage path, weighted by where it lands on the curve. Scarcity expected in the steep region counts enormously; the same tonnage in the flat region counts for nothing.

Two more facts we will need. The coverage path's \emph{shape} is essentially public --- published deficit forecasts $D(t,h)$ pin its slope, $\partial_h Y \approx -D/q$ --- while its \emph{level} is latent. And It\^o applied to \eqref{eq:price} ties price variance to the path:
\begin{equation}
\sigma_P^2 \;=\; \sigma_A^2 \;+\; \big\|f'\big(Y(t,\cdot)\big)\big\|^2_{\Sigma_M} + \text{cross terms}.
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
In English: volatility is anchor noise plus forecast-revision noise amplified by the slope of the storage curve. Thin coverage, steep slope, big variance. Comfortable coverage, flat slope, and volatility floors at whatever the anchor is doing.

Finally, what does it cost to hold $x$ units between trading opportunities?
\begin{equation}
c(x) \;=\; \underbrace{k\,P\,|x|}_{\text{warehouse}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{\gamma_r}{2}\,\sigma_P^2\,P^2 x^2}_{\text{risk and capital}} \;+\; \text{funding},
\qquad k \approx 20\ \text{bps/yr}.
\label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
With $k$ negligible the potential is a parabola whose curvature is set by risk, not rent. Keep an eye on that: the physical world contributes a linear term too small to matter, and everything that matters is quadratic. This is the first sense in which low carry ``leans quadratic,'' and via \eqref{eq:vol} the curvature is set by the state of the system itself. The market grades its own homework.

\section{The dealer already solved this}\label{sec:dealer}

Recall the sealed-bid dealer model. Enquiries arrive Poisson with mean gap $\tau$; each is a sell with probability $p$, a buy with probability $1-p$; the best competing response beyond fair value is exponential with mean $w$ (the \emph{market width}; hazard $h = 1/w$); winning a trade costs $\epsilon$ of adverse selection; size is $s$. The whole policy is characterized by an inventory indifference cost $\nu(x)$, through its discrete slope and convexity
\[
S(x) = \frac{\nu(x+s)-\nu(x-s)}{2s}, \qquad
C(x) = \frac{\nu(x+s)-2\nu(x)+\nu(x-s)}{2s}.
\]
The optimal quotes are then
\begin{equation}
\text{half-width}(x) = \Delta + C(x), \qquad
\text{mid} = p^{\text{market}} - S(x), \qquad \Delta = \tfrac1h + \epsilon .
\label{eq:quotes}
\end{equation}
The old slogans still hold: \emph{slope of inventory cost = skew; convexity of inventory cost = (discretionary) width}; markup = market width + adverse selection + marginal inventory cost. The two-sided, interior consistency equation can be written in centered form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\tau\, c(x)}{s}
= \frac{e^{-1-h\epsilon}}{h}\,
\left[
 e^{-hC(x)}
 \left\{p\,e^{-hS(x)}+(1-p)e^{hS(x)}\right\}
-\big(\text{same expression at }x=0\big)
\right].
\label{eq:bellman}
\end{equation}
This representation is local to states where both submitted markups are positive. On a finite inventory lattice the one-sided boundary Bellman rows are part of the problem; the symmetry below scales those rows term by term as well.

The algebraic identity is
\begin{equation}
p\,e^{-hS}+(1-p)e^{hS}
= D(p)\cosh\!\big(h(S-\delta)\big),\qquad
D(p)=2\sqrt{p(1-p)},\qquad
\delta=\frac{1}{2h}\log\frac{p}{1-p}.
\label{eq:imbalanceidentity}
\end{equation}
Write $\gamma=h^{-1}\log(1/D(p))$ and $M(p)=1/D(p)=e^{h\gamma}$.

\begin{proposition}[Imbalance is a clock change and cost multiplier]\label{prop:multiplier}
On a finite inventory lattice, assume every available quote in the imbalanced problem and its balanced image is interior. If $(\rho_p,\nu_p)$ solves the problem with seller probability $p$ and carrying cost $c$, then
\[
\bar\nu(x)=\nu_p(x)-\delta x
\]
solves the balanced problem with carrying cost $M(p)c$ and average reward $M(p)\rho_p$. Conversely, every such balanced solution maps back. Hence $S_p=\bar S+\delta$ and $C_p=\bar C$. At every two-sided state, the physical quote width is the width of the balanced dealer at cost $M(p)c$; it is not widened by an additional $\gamma$.
\end{proposition}

Indeed, with $G(K)=h^{-1}e^{-1-hK}$ on the interior branch,
\[
p\,G(\bar K^\downarrow+\delta)
=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\,G(\bar K^\downarrow),\qquad
(1-p)\,G(\bar K^\uparrow-\delta)
=\sqrt{p(1-p)}\,G(\bar K^\uparrow).
\]
The same termwise factor applies when one direction is unavailable at a boundary. Thus the imbalanced Hamiltonian is $D(p)$ times the balanced one; multiplying the full average-reward equation by $M(p)$ proves the proposition.

The parameter $\gamma$ is only the same clock rescaling expressed in markup units:
$D(p)e^{-hm}=e^{-h(m+\gamma)}$. One may use this artificial overhead frame \emph{or} the cost-multiplier frame, but not apply both as separate physical corrections. In the cost frame the observable width response is
\begin{equation}
W_{p,c}(x)-W_{1/2,c}(x)
=2\left[C_{1/2,M(p)c}(x)-C_{1/2,c}(x)\right],
\label{eq:widthresponse}
\end{equation}
where both sides are available. Its size and sign depend on the balanced solution's cost sensitivity. The multiplier amplifies a positive calendar-time cost per effective inventory transition, but
\[
c\equiv0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad M(p)c\equiv0:
\]
imbalance does not manufacture carrying cost from nothing.

Storage economics contains no flow multiplier of this form. The exact statement is nevertheless narrower than the original slogan: imbalance slows the controlled inventory clock and changes the policy through any cost already present; whether dealer feedback supplies a separate stabilizer is a further modelling question.

\section{What constant width does and does not imply}\label{sec:cwls}

The finite differences $S$ and $C$ come from the same potential and cannot be chosen independently. Directly from their definitions,
\begin{equation}
S(x+s)-S(x)=C(x+s)+C(x).
\label{eq:compatibility}
\end{equation}
Thus $C(x)\equiv C_0$ forces $S$ to be affine on the inventory lattice. If in addition $S_\delta(0)=0$ and the centered interior equation \eqref{eq:bellman} applies, the compatible carrying cost must have the form
\begin{equation}
c(x)=\frac{s\,D(p)e^{-1-h\epsilon-hC_0}}{\tau h}
\left[\cosh\!\big(hS_\delta(x)\big)-1\right],
\qquad S_\delta(x)\ \text{affine}.
\label{eq:cwlscost}
\end{equation}
It is therefore cosh-shaped, not an arbitrary linear-plus-quadratic cost.

Holding $C=C_0$ fixed and inverting the centered equation pointwise gives only
\[
\left|S_\delta(x)\right|
=\frac1h\cosh^{-1}\!\left(
1+\frac{\tau h\,e^{1+h\epsilon+hC_0}}{sD(p)}\,c(x)
\right).
\]
The absolute value leaves a branch choice, and the resulting values need not satisfy
\eqref{eq:compatibility}. This inversion is a useful reduced-form local heuristic, not a construction of an optimal dealer value function for arbitrary $c$.

For a genuinely constant-width solution, \eqref{eq:cwlscost} is locally quadratic because
$\cosh z-1=z^2/2+O(z^4)$; this explains why linear skew and quadratic cost can agree to leading order near the anchor. A linear warehouse term may still alter the endogenously solved quote surface, but the earlier square-root-kink argument did not establish how. Determining whether a kink survives after solving jointly for $S$ and $C$ is an open comparative-static problem.

\begin{remark}[Why FX dealers do not hoard]
Low physical carry is not sufficient. An FX dealer also pays essentially nothing to hold, yet runs her book flat in minutes (Lyons 1995) because she has an interdealer market to trade out through and overnight capital charges that amount to high effective carry. Where an exit market exists, dealers manage inventory by trading rather than quoting, and the skew signal goes quiet. The explosive-capable regime proposed here is therefore a conjunction: negligible physical carry, quiet volatility, soft funding, and \emph{no interdealer exit}. This remains a hypothesis about the coupled market, not a consequence of Proposition~\ref{prop:multiplier}.
\end{remark}

The corrected separation matters for the wider program. The quote identities still reveal the first two finite differences of the dealer's inventory value, and the exact imbalance map still says how to compare flow regimes. They do not by themselves prove that the dealer layer replaces a missing physical holding cost, that CWLS is globally exact for a quadratic cost, or that inventory can run away. Those conclusions require a model that closes the storage, dealer and price-feedback layers and establishes its stability properties.

\section{The oscillator}\label{sec:oscillator}

Now close the loop. Linearize: let $y$ be the coverage gap, let consumption respond to the price gap with elasticity $\varepsilon_d$ and lag $T$, and notice that the dealer layer puts the skew --- which responds to \emph{flow} through dealer books --- into the transacted price:
\begin{equation}
p - a \;=\; -\,\varphi\, y \;-\; \psi\, \dot y ,
\qquad \varphi \propto f'(Y^*),
\qquad \psi \propto \nu'' \propto \gamma_r\, \sigma_P^2 .
\label{eq:pricegap}
\end{equation}
Read it as mechanics. The storage curve contributes the spring, $\varphi$. The dealer layer contributes the \emph{dashpot}, $\psi$: a velocity-dependent, dissipative term. Substitute into the consumption dynamics and you get
\begin{equation}
T\,\ddot y \;+\; \big(1 + \varepsilon_d\, \psi\big)\,\dot y \;+\; \varepsilon_d\, \varphi\, y \;=\; \text{forcing},
\label{eq:oscillator}
\end{equation}
while an extrapolative anchor, $\dot a = \beta \times (\text{trend of } p)$ --- Weymar's one positive feedback loop --- enters as \emph{negative} damping. Three things follow.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em]
\item \textbf{What the low-carry limit removes.} Sending the warehouse coefficient $k$ to zero removes the linear $|x|$ term, but it does not remove the positive quadratic risk or funding terms in \eqref{eq:cost}. Nor is this the classical cheap-control limit, which sends an \emph{input} penalty to zero. Vanishing state cost can weaken inventory mean reversion, but it proves neither an impulsive policy nor explosive inventory. Boundedness, recurrence and any turnpike rate must be derived from the complete controlled dynamics and boundary conditions.
\item \textbf{A possible amplitude-dependent damping channel.} Equation~\eqref{eq:vol} suggests that forecast revisions can raise $\sigma_P^2$ in stressed states, and hence raise a risk-based contribution to $\psi$. If a closed model establishes that dependence, if extrapolative feedback subtracts enough damping near the origin, and if net damping becomes positive at larger amplitude, then a Van der Pol--type mechanism is plausible:
\begin{equation}
T\,\ddot y + \big[\mu(\text{amplitude})-\tilde\beta\big]\,\dot y
+\varepsilon_d\varphi\,y=\text{noise}.
\label{eq:vdp}
\end{equation}
A sign-changing damping coefficient alone does not prove a limit cycle; existence, uniqueness and attraction require additional conditions on the nonlinear restoring and damping terms.
\item \textbf{The edge is a feedback conjecture.} The proposed loop is: quiet markets lower volatility-based cost, weaker cost softens inventory control, and a disturbance raises risk cost and dealer resistance. Any positive risk or funding charge is magnified per effective inventory transition by $M(p)$, and the resulting quote response may add damping. If $c\equiv0$, the multiplier channel is absent. Whether this loop selects marginal stability, a stationary law, a cycle or divergence is the stability question, not a consequence of the dealer symmetry.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{conjecture}[Stability boundary]\label{conj:boundary}
In the coupled system \eqref{eq:storage}--\eqref{eq:oscillator} there is a critical surface in $(k, \beta, \varepsilon_d, g, \kappa)$ --- physical carry, extrapolation gain, demand elasticity, forecast quality, dealer risk capital --- separating stationary from explosive inventory, and $k \approx 20$\,bps sits on the unstable side \emph{absent} the endogenous channels above. With them, the generic attractor is a limit cycle whose amplitude grows with dealer capacity $\kappa$.
\end{conjecture}

\section{What exactly is being claimed}\label{sec:hypotheses}

The argument bundles a dozen claims that deserve separate fates. Unbundled --- each stated so that it can be false:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=3.7em, style=nextline]
\item[H1 (No spring.)] In quiet regimes, low-carry prices have no mean reversion: variance ratios at multi-year horizons sit at or above~1, where high-carry commodities sit well below.
\item[H2 (Hoard spikes.)] Large moves are asymmetrically to the upside, and the asymmetry is ordered by carry class: low $>$ mid $>$ high.
\item[H3 (Tails.)] Low-carry return tails are fatter than mid-carry exchange-traded commodities'. (Note what this does \emph{not} claim: grains can be just as fat --- they spike through the stockout boundary. The distinctive low-carry signature is the \emph{conjunction} of fat tails with H1's absent reversion.)
\item[H4 (Hoards happen.)] Inventory equal to \emph{years} of production actually accumulates in low-carry goods, without a price-mediated correction along the way.
\item[H5 (Boring spreads.)] In quiet regimes, low-carry dealer spreads are tight and approximately insensitive to inventory.
\item[H6 (The kink.)] After $S$ and $C$ are solved jointly subject to \eqref{eq:compatibility}, dealer skew near zero inventory changes detectably when a linear warehouse charge is added to quadratic risk cost. The earlier square-root formula was a pointwise heuristic and does not establish this hypothesis.
\item[H7 (The multiplier.)] Persistent one-sided flow shifts skew by $\delta$ and maps the dealer to the balanced problem at cost $M(p)c$. The width response is the cost-sensitive quantity in \eqref{eq:widthresponse}, not a universal multiplication of physical width.
\item[H8 (Width leads.)] Quoted width leads realized volatility, and width/skew lead the price break around episodes --- quotes are forward-looking measurements of the same latent state.
\item[H9 (Financing ends it.)] Hoard episodes terminate as financing or width events (credit withdrawn, quotes blown out, markets suspended) --- never because carrying cost bit.
\item[H10 (Tails deter.)] Fat width tails deter hoards ex ante.
\item[H11 (Degenerate limit.)] At effectively infinite coverage (the canonical store-of-value good), the storage term vanishes entirely and price is pure anchor dynamics.
\item[H12 (The conjunction.)] Zero physical carry alone is not sufficient for explosive capability; it also requires quiet volatility, soft funding, and no interdealer exit market.
\end{description}

Honest reading of the table: the price-series and episode hypotheses (H1, H2, H4, H9, H11) are supported; one hypothesis died and was rebuilt (H10); the quote-surface hypotheses that are \emph{distinctively ours} (H6, H7, and the sharp half of H8) are untested --- not for want of trying, but because nobody has ever assembled a bid--offer width history for an assessed market. That is the dataset to build.

\section{Where this sits}

The storage half is Weymar (1965, 1968): price as a functional of the expected coverage path, anchored by an extrapolative long-run expectation. Every stationarity theorem in the competitive-storage line (Gustafson; Scheinkman--Schechtman; Deaton--Laroque) leans on the carry term this note deletes; Bobenrieth--Bobenrieth--Wright (2013) get explosive price runs inside rational storage with carry intact, and the quantity-side question at $k \approx 0$ appears open. The dealer half is Ho--Stoll through Avellaneda--Stoikov and Gu\'eant, with Cotton--Papanicolaou as the sealed-bid variant carrying an explicit cost of carry --- the term doing all the work here. Duffie--Dworczak--Zhu (2017) showed a benchmark's \emph{level} reveals dealer information in OTC search markets; the present claim is that the benchmark's width and asymmetry do the revealing, because they are the second and first derivatives of the market's storage value function. The singular limit is cheap control; the explosion diagnostic is turnpike loss; the cycle is Van der Pol's, with the dissipation quoted twice a week.
