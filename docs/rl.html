Learning faster with the symmetry

Reinforcement learning and its inverse, with and without the imbalance equivalence. Companion to On a Simple Relationship Between Order Imbalance, Skew and Width in Over-The-Counter Trading, §6.

The flow regime cycles through four imbalances (q = 0.40, 0.50, 0.60, 0.70, switching every 400 enquiries). Two identical tabular Q-learners quote into this market. The baseline learns a separate policy per regime — each table sees a quarter of the data. The symmetry learner keeps one policy in the balanced frame and maps it through the skew shift δ(q) = (w/2) log(q/(1−q)): every regime’s experience trains the same table. The correction is exact to first order in q − ½; the residual γ and M(q) effects are second order, and show up only as a small asymptotic gap. The oracle quotes the exact optimal policy.

Look for: the symmetry learner reaching in ten thousand enquiries a level the baseline has not reached in eighty thousand. Same algorithm, same stream — the invariance is the only difference.

The inverse problem: reading a dealer’s cost from her quotes

Now watch instead of act. A dealer quotes optimally across the same four regimes, and an observer sees noisy quotes only. The observer wants her skew curve S(x) — by the paper’s identities, the slope of her inventory cost. The per-regime estimator uses only observations from the target regime. The pooled estimator de-tilts every observation by δ(q) into the balanced frame, so all four regimes’ quotes estimate one curve.

Look for: pooling cutting the estimation error severalfold at realistic sample sizes — and, at very large samples, the pooled error hitting a floor. That floor is the second-order bias of the first-order-exact symmetry: the approximate symmetry, made visible.
